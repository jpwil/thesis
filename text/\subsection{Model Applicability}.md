\subsection{Model Applicability}

If a model demonstrates good performance and is constructed using reasonable assumptions, its predictions should be applicable to the patient populations and settings used for model development and/or updating\cite{moons2025}. This raises an important question: ``How similar are the patients used in model development to the intended target population?''

In patients with VL/HIV co-infection, or other forms of severe immunosuppression, treatment failure or relapse is considered the norm rather than the exception\cite{abongomera2017a}. As all models presented here were developed in immunocompetent patients, extrapolation to immunosuppressed populations would likely underestimate true relapse risk and result in poor calibration. These models should therefore \textit{not} be used in this high-risk group.

Patients with severe disease were largely excluded from the contributing clinical trials, and model performance in this subgroup is therefore uncertain. On the one hand, severe disease may be associated with a higher risk of relapse despite an initial treatment response. These patients are likely to be more immunosuppressed as a consequence of disease severity, and therefore more likely to relapse. Furthermore, severe disease itself may share risk factors for relapse, including immunosuppression (malnutrition, immune-naivety), or infection with more virulent parasite strains\cite{rai2013}. On the other hand, severe disease may be associated with a lower relapse risk. In many settings, these patients receive more intensive management, including extended treatment regimens and preferential use of liposomal amphotericin B. They may also undergo post-treatment tissue aspiration to confirm cure. Given these competing considerations, the use of the presented models in patients with severe disease should be avoided.

A further important limitation is the definition of initial cure in the development datasets, how this differs from routine clinical practice, and how this may lead to systematic underestimation of true 6-month relapse risk. As discussed in the Methodology (Section~\ref{sec:meth_initial_cure}), contributing studies used a range of methods to assess initial cure, often including repeat clinical and biochemical assessment with tissue aspiration approximately one month after treatment initiation (full details are provided in the Supplementary Material). In trial settings, patients who failed this assessment either received extended treatment with follow-up evaluation or were classified as having initial treatment failure and excluded from subsequent analyses. In routine practice, however, the definition of initial cure is less clearly defined, leading to inclusion of patients who would not meet formal trial criteria and consequently inflating observed relapse risk. 

For example, in the ISC, where most patients receive single-dose liposomal amphotericin B and are discharged on the same day, follow-up within one month typically occurs only if patients re-present with persistent or recurrent symptoms. In contrast, in East Africa, first-line treatment involves a 17-day course of injections, allowing for clinical assessment at the end of treatment, but without routine tissue aspiration as performed in trial settings. The extent to which these differences affect model applicability is uncertain and likely varies across settings.

Additional concerns arise when patients are treated with novel or non-standard regimens not represented in the development data. For example, updated WHO guidelines are expected to introduce paromomycin and miltefosine as treatment options in East Africa\cite{musa2023}.

In summary, the models are most applicable to patients with non-severe disease, without significant immunosuppression, and treated with regimens similar to those used in model development. External validation using independent datasets, ideally with prospective real-world follow-up, is required to further assess model applicability and performance.
