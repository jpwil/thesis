Visceral leishmaniasis (VL) is a neglected parasitic disease that disproportionately affects the poorest individuals. Elimination efforts have been underway in the Indian subcontinent (ISC) for over twenty years, and have recently launched in East Africa. VL relapse is a common event that presents a growing threat to elimination through the persistence of high-risk infection reservoirs. In this thesis, I aim to enhance our understanding of which patients relapse and why, thereby contributing to improved elimination strategies. This is achieved through transparent and robust statistical methods in prediction modelling, applied to individual participant data (IPD) from clinical trials.

In Chapter~\ref{ch:1-background} I introduce the disease and its epidemiology. I summarise the existing, yet fragmentary, evidence on VL relapse and explain how relapse constitutes an underrecognised challenge to elimination.

A systematic review of VL prediction models is presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:2-sys-review}. I show that no prediction models for VL relapse have been developed, and that existing tools for VL mortality prediction are at high risk of bias. I argue that developing a prediction model for VL relapse is an urgent priority.

In Chapter~\ref{ch:3-isc-model-methodology} I describe the methods used to acquire, harmonise, and analyse IPD from VL clinical trials. Terminology and concepts related to prediction modelling are introduced, and the rationale for chosen methods is explained. Particular attention is paid to the handling of missing data, how between-study heterogeneity is accounted for, and model performance evaluation.

The harmonised datasets and VL relapse prediction models are summarised in Chapters~\ref{ch:4-isc-model-results} (ISC) and \ref{ch:5-ea-model-results} (East Africa). For each region, two prediction models are presented: one including, and one excluding parasite grade. Relapse predictors identified across both regions include (i) shorter symptom durations and (ii) higher parasite grades at diagnosis. In East Africa, malnutrition and anaemia severity are also associated with increased relapse risk.

In Chapter~\ref{ch:6-discussion}, I discuss the patient and public health implications of the model findings. A causal model is postulated to explain the identified relapse predictors. I hypothesise that these predictors act as proxies for underlying immunological mechanisms driving relapse risk.

In summary, this thesis presents the largest study to date on the drivers of VL relapse, and the first to develop prediction models for VL relapse. The clinically focussed findings have important implications for the management of VL patients and the design of elimination strategies.
